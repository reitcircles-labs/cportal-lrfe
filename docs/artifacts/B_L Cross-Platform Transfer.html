B/L Cross-Platform Transfer
Design for review · epic CB-8 · ticket CB-17

Sending a B/L to another eBL platform, and receiving one

Not every bank or buyer will use our platform. This flow moves a bill of lading to a holder on another electronic B/L platform, and takes one in, the way the DCSA platform interoperability standard (PINT) describes: one envelope carries the B/L and its whole transfer history, the receiving platform says yes or no, and the sending platform's copy stops counting the moment it says yes.

What this flow assumes

CB-9's five decisions are not agreed yet. This page assumes its recommendations: the receiver accepts a transfer; UK Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 as the reference law (a country setting); the forwarder does not hold title in v1; a neutral operator; the eleven personas.

  • Still exactly one holder. Across two platforms, "one holder" means one platform has the B/L active. While it travels, it stays active here; once the other platform confirms, our copy is inactive for good.
  • Title moves only by the holder's endorsement, never by the operator, and never because a message got lost: no answer means no move.
  • Our blockchain proves what happened here: that we sent it, to whom, and that the other side confirmed; or that we received it, with the full history it came with. Steps recorded on the other platform are proved by that platform's signature, not by our blockchain.

Marked to confirm: PINT details and legal points I am not sure of. They are checked against the published DCSA PINT specification (version 3.x at the time of writing, to confirm) before CB-17 is built.

Part 2

How a transfer between platforms works

In the PINT model the platforms talk to each other; the users each stay on their own platform. What I am sure of, and what is to confirm:

  • An envelope. The sending platform sends one envelope holding the B/L document, a manifest signed by the sending platform, and the envelope transfer chain: the list of every step since issue (issue, transfers, endorsements), each entry signed by the platform that recorded it and linked to the one before by a checksum. The receiver can check the whole history without asking anyone.
  • Supporting documents can follow the envelope one by one before the sender says the transfer is finished. to confirm the exact steps (start, documents, finish) of the version we build on.
  • An answer. The receiving platform checks the envelope and answers with a code. The ones I am fairly sure of: RECE received and accepted, DUPE already received earlier, BSIG a signature failed, BENV the envelope is wrong. to confirm the full list (for example a "disputed" answer) and whether the answer itself is signed.
  • Our copy becomes inactive on RECE or DUPE; on any other answer, or none, the B/L stays active here.
  • Finding the receiver. A party is named by a code and the code list it comes from (the receiving platform's own party code, or a global identifier such as an LEI). PINT has a call to ask the receiving platform who a party code belongs to before sending. to confirm its exact form and which code lists each platform accepts.

Part 7

Chain of title across platforms

  • One history, two kinds of proof. The chain that travels in the envelope is the B/L's history. We keep every chain we send or receive, unchanged, with its signatures. CB-16's history screen shows each entry with the platform that recorded it (sketch 4).
  • Our entries are proved on our blockchain, as for any title step. The other platform's entries are proved by its signature, checked with the key we hold for it. We do not anchor their steps one by one; we anchor the checksum of the whole chain as we received it, which proves what we got and when.
  • When the B/L comes back, the chain contains our earlier entries. They must match our records to the byte, or the transfer is refused. That makes a rewritten history visible.
  • While the B/L is away our history ends at "Sent out". We do not learn the steps on the other platform until the B/L comes back. Henrik's evidence pack for a B/L that never comes back has our part and the other platform's confirmation; the rest he gets from that platform.
  • Public check (CB-15) for a B/L that is away: "Held on Meridian eBL since 12 Nov 2026. This platform's copy is no longer valid." It never shows the copy as current.
Part 8

Trust between platforms

  • A connection is set up before any transfer. The operator adds a platform to the directory: its name, its platform code, its address, its public signing keys, and the date of the agreement with it. A second operator person approves (four-eyes). The keys are fixed in the directory; a key change is a new approval. to confirm how DCSA expects keys to be exchanged and whether a shared registry exists.
  • A legal agreement between the two platforms covers who answers for what (lost messages, wrong receiver, a disputed history). Its form is to confirm; groups such as the DCSA members and the Digital Standards Initiative work on shared terms.
  • Our signing key for chain entries and envelopes is kept like the platform's other keys, and rotated with notice to connected platforms.
  • Legal standing. The ETDA asks for a reliable system that gives exclusive control. Whether a copy made inactive on a confirmed answer meets that across two platforms is to confirm with counsel. Carriers' P&I cover for electronic B/Ls is usually tied to systems approved by the International Group of P&I Clubs; whether transfers to another platform need anything more is to confirm.
  • No vendor names on our screens other than the receiving platform's own name, which the user needs to pick it. Technical names (PINT, the response codes) stay out of user screens; the codes appear only in the operator's connections log.

Part 11

Decisions for you

A recommendation is given; tell me where you disagree.

1. How does a receiver here accept a B/L from another platform?

  • A: Our platform answers once the automatic checks pass; the receiver then keeps it or returns it.
  • B: Our platform answers only after the receiver accepts (needs a delayed answer in PINT, to confirm).
  • C: Receivers set "accept automatically from other platforms" in advance; no step for the person.

Recommended: A. It works with an immediate answer, keeps CB-9's "the receiver decides", and matches UCP 600 practice (refused documents are returned).

2. What if the other platform never gives a definite answer?

  • A: Keep the B/L locked, retry the same envelope, and after 24 hours open an exception case; unlock only on a refusal from that platform, or its written statement that it never received the B/L, recorded with four-eyes.
  • B: Unlock automatically after a time limit.

Recommended: A. B risks two holders when only the answer was lost. A rare event, so it must not slow down the normal path.

3. Who connects another platform?

  • A: The operator, with four-eyes, only after a signed agreement and a test transfer.
  • B: Each organisation adds the platforms it works with.

Recommended: A. Trust in a platform's keys is a platform-wide matter; organisations only pick from the list.

4. Supporting documents in the first version?

  • A: The B/L only.
  • B: The B/L plus documents attached to it (invoice, packing list, certificates).

Recommended: A for v1, B later if banks on the other side ask for it. Letter-of-credit documents usually travel by the banking channel anyway.

5. Which other platform do we test against first?

  • A: A second install of our own platform acting as "the other platform".
  • B: A real eBL platform's test environment, with its agreement.

Recommended: A first, then B before calling CB-17 done: our own pair proves the flow; a real platform proves we read PINT the same way.
